Artifacts, Quality Assurance and Safety

Artifacts are not failures of the machine; they are the physics of the preceding fourteen chapters becoming visible. Almost every one can be reasoned out from first principles, which is far more useful than memorising a list. The second half of this chapter is about the ways MRI can injure people — a subject where the physics is elegant and the consequences are not.

The diagnostic algorithm

1. Which direction does it run? Along phase encode → motion or wrap. Along readout → chemical shift or bandwidth. Diagonal → k-space spike.
2. Is it periodic? Discrete repeating copies → periodic motion or N/2 mismatch. Diffuse smear → random motion.
3. Where is it worst? Near air or metal → susceptibility. In the image centre → g-factor. At the FOV edge → gradient non-linearity or coil falloff.
4. Does it change with sequence? Present on GRE but not SE → T2*/susceptibility. Present on every sequence and every patient → hardware; call service.

15.4  Safety

MRI uses no ionising radiation, and that fact leads people to describe it as "safe". It is not safe; it is differently dangerous. There are four independent hazard mechanisms, and each has killed or injured people.

The static field: projectiles and implants

The one rule that matters most

The magnet is always on. Not during the scan — always, including nights, weekends, power cuts and building works. Every serious MRI incident in the literature involves someone who either did not know this or forgot it in a hurry. The Emergency Run Down Unit exists to quench the magnet when a person is pinned; it is used perhaps once in a scanner's lifetime, costs a great deal, and is the correct decision every time it is needed.

Key points

  • Artifacts are deducible. Direction, periodicity, location and sequence-dependence identify almost all of them.
  • Ghosts and wrap follow the phase-encode axis; chemical shift follows the readout axis.
  • Susceptibility artifacts are worst on gradient echo with long TE and are managed with SE, short TE and high bandwidth.
  • Routine QA catches scanner-side problems before they reach patients; a single dead coil element is invisible clinically but obvious on QA.
  • Four hazard mechanisms: static field (projectiles, implants), RF (heating), gradients (nerve stimulation, noise), cryogens (asphyxiation).
  • Burns are the most common injury, and they are almost entirely preventable by positioning and padding.
