Fast Imaging: Parallel, Compressed Sensing, Learned Reconstruction

Scan time is TR × NPE × NEX, and patients cannot hold still forever. Every acceleration method answers the same question — how do you skip most of k-space and still get an image? — with a different source of extra information: coil geometry, sparsity, or a learned prior. Both demos in this chapter run the real algorithms in your browser.

14.2  Parallel imaging: coil geometry as information

Undersample k-space by a factor R along the phase-encode direction and the FOV shrinks by R, so R different locations fold onto the same pixel. In a single-coil image that is fatal: one measurement, R unknowns. But with C receive coils you get C measurements of that same pixel, each weighted by that coil's sensitivity at each of the R folded locations. If C ≥ R, and if the coil sensitivities differ enough, it is an ordinary system of linear equations.

14.1 a = S ρ   ⟹   ρ̂ = (SHS)−1SH a      gρ = √[ (SHS)−1ρρ · (SHS)ρρ ] ≥ 1 a holds the C aliased pixel values, S is the C×R sensitivity matrix, ρ the R true pixel values. The g-factor measures how ill-conditioned that matrix is at each pixel: SNR falls by √R from the missing samples and by a further factor g from the unfolding itself. Where coil sensitivities are similar — deep in the centre of the body, far from every element — g rises sharply, which is why parallel imaging artifacts appear first in the middle of the image.

Simultaneous multi-slice — the acceleration that is nearly free

Excite several slices at once with a multiband RF pulse, then separate them afterwards using coil sensitivity variation along the slice direction. Because you are not skipping k-space samples, the √R noise penalty of in-plane acceleration does not apply; only the g-factor cost remains, and CAIPIRINHA phase shifts reduce even that by shifting the simultaneous slices relative to one another. This is why modern diffusion and fMRI protocols routinely run multiband factors of 3–8 and acquire whole-brain volumes in well under a second.

14.3  Compressed sensing: sparsity as information

MR images are compressible — a JPEG or wavelet representation throws away most coefficients with no visible loss. Compressed sensing asks the obvious follow-up: if the image is mostly zeros in some transform, why acquire full data and then discard it? Three conditions must hold:

  1. Sparsity. The image has a concise representation in some transform (wavelets, finite differences, or across time for dynamic imaging, where it is dramatic).
  2. Incoherent sampling. Undersample randomly, not regularly. Regular undersampling produces coherent replicas — crisp ghosts that look exactly like real anatomy. Random undersampling produces diffuse, noise-like interference which a denoising algorithm can distinguish from structure.
  3. Non-linear reconstruction. Solve min ‖Ψx‖₁ subject to the acquired samples, iteratively — no closed-form inverse exists.
The honest caveat about learned reconstruction

Replacing the hand-designed sparsity prior with a neural network trained on thousands of image pairs (variational networks, unrolled optimisation, diffusion-model priors) produces sharper images at higher accelerations, and is now shipping on clinical scanners under names like Deep Resolve, AIR Recon DL and SmartSpeed. The unresolved concern is that a strong learned prior can synthesise plausible structure that was never measured — and the failure mode is not noisy and obvious, it is clean and confident. The fastMRI challenge found reconstructions with excellent SSIM scores that nonetheless removed or invented small pathology. Evaluate these methods on lesion detection, not on pixel-wise similarity metrics.

Key points

  • Undersampling causes aliasing; acceleration methods differ only in what extra information they use to undo it.
  • Parallel imaging uses coil sensitivity differences; SNR falls by √R × g, and g is worst where coils overlap most.
  • SENSE unfolds in image space; GRAPPA interpolates in k-space; their artifacts look different.
  • Simultaneous multi-slice avoids the √R penalty because no k-space samples are skipped.
  • Compressed sensing needs sparsity plus incoherent sampling plus non-linear recon — all three, or it fails.
  • Learned reconstruction is powerful and requires task-based validation, not similarity metrics.
