Part IV · Sequences and Contrast

Pulse Sequences: The Catalogue

Every sequence is built from four verbs — excite, refocus, encode, read — arranged on a schedule. The bewildering list of acronyms is three vendors naming the same dozen arrangements differently. This chapter organises them by what they actually do, so a new acronym becomes a question of "which family?" rather than a memory test.

11.1  The family tree

11.2  The spin echo family

Conventional spin echo

90° – 180° – read, one line of k-space per TR. Immune to field inhomogeneity, excellent image quality, and hopelessly slow: 256 lines × 500 ms = over two minutes for a single slice group. It survives as the reference standard and for T1-weighted work where TR is short anyway.

Turbo / fast spin echo (TSE, FSE, RARE)

Apply a CPMG train of refocusing pulses after one excitation, and give each echo a different phase-encoding amplitude. The scan speeds up by the echo train length (ETL, also called turbo factor). Three consequences follow immediately:

  • Effective TE. The echoes are not all equal — each has decayed by a different amount. The contrast of the image is set by whichever echo fills the centre of k-space, and that time is called the effective TE. Everything in Chapter 8 about the centre carrying contrast is what makes this work.
  • Blurring. The later echoes are weaker, so k-space is multiplied by a decaying envelope along the phase direction — which convolves the image with the transform of that envelope. Longer trains mean more blurring, particularly of tissues with short T2.
  • Bright fat. The rapid train of 180° pulses disrupts the J-coupling that normally shortens the T2 of lipid protons, so fat stays bright on T2-weighted TSE in a way it never was on conventional SE. This is why fat suppression became routine when TSE did.

11.3  Inversion recovery: nulling a tissue on purpose

Precede the sequence with a 180° inversion pulse and wait a time TI before exciting. Each tissue's longitudinal magnetization recovers from −M₀ through zero to +M₀ at its own T1 rate. Excite at the moment a particular tissue passes through zero and that tissue contributes no signal at all.

11.4  The gradient echo family

Spoiled gradient echo (FLASH, SPGR, T1-FFE)

Small flip angle, short TR, and any leftover transverse magnetization deliberately destroyed ("spoiled") before the next repetition by a gradient crusher plus RF phase cycling. Because only a small fraction of Mz is used each time, TR can be a few milliseconds and the whole 3D volume acquired in a breath-hold.

11.2 S = M₀ sin α · (1 − E₁)/(1 − cos α · E₁) · e−TE/T2*,   E₁ = e−TR/T1   ⟹   αErnst = arccos(E₁) The Ernst angle maximises signal for a given TR and T1. Below it you waste available magnetization; above it you saturate. Because it depends on T1, choosing a flip angle is simultaneously choosing which tissue is favoured — flip angle is a contrast control in GRE just as TR is in spin echo.

Balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP: TrueFISP, FIESTA, balanced FFE)

Instead of spoiling residual transverse magnetization, keep it — every gradient in every axis is balanced so that the net phase accrued per TR is zero, and the magnetization reaches a steady state containing both longitudinal and transverse components. The result is a remarkably high-SNR image with a very unusual contrast, proportional to T2/T1. Fluid and fat are bright; nearly everything else is grey.

Banding artifact — the price of balance

bSSFP requires the phase per TR to be identical everywhere. Where B₀ is off resonance by 1/(2·TR), the steady state destructively interferes and a black band appears across the image. This is why bSSFP demands excellent shimming and the shortest possible TR, why the bands move when you re-shim, and why bSSFP is popular in cardiac imaging (short TR, good shim over the heart) but awkward at 3 T and above.

11.5  EPI: the whole image in one shot

After a single excitation, oscillate the readout gradient to traverse k-space in a zig-zag, blipping the phase gradient between lines. A 64×64 image in 30–60 ms; a whole brain in 2 seconds. This is the only practical way to freeze diffusion, perfusion or neural activity, so essentially all of Chapter 13 rests on it. Its weaknesses are severe and characteristic:

  • Geometric distortion. The effective bandwidth along the phase-encode direction is tiny (the whole train, not one line), so even small off-resonance shifts pixels by centimetres near sinuses and ear canals. Corrected with a field map or a reverse-phase-encode acquisition (topup).
  • N/2 ghosting. Odd and even lines are read in opposite directions; any timing or eddy-current mismatch between them produces a ghost displaced by half the FOV.
  • T2* blurring and dropout. The readout lasts tens of milliseconds, comparable with T2*, so signal decays across k-space — and near air interfaces it disappears entirely.
  • Chemical shift. Fat is displaced by a large fraction of the FOV, so fat suppression is mandatory, not optional.

Key points

  • Three questions identify any sequence: how the echo forms, what prepares the magnetization, and how k-space is ordered.
  • TSE trades speed for blurring, and its contrast is set by the effective TE — the echo that fills central k-space.
  • TI = 0.693 × T1 nulls a tissue. STIR nulls fat, FLAIR nulls CSF; the sequences are otherwise the same idea.
  • In gradient echo, flip angle is a contrast parameter, and the Ernst angle sets the maximum.
  • bSSFP gives T2/T1 contrast and superb SNR, at the cost of banding wherever the shim fails.
  • EPI buys speed with distortion, ghosting and dropout — and enables all of functional and diffusion imaging.
  • Fat suppression has four different mechanisms; they fail in different circumstances, which is why all four survive.
