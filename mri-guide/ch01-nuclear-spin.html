Nuclear Spin and the Magnetic Moment

Every MRI image is, at bottom, a map of hydrogen nuclei that were briefly persuaded to broadcast radio waves. Before any of that can happen, a proton has to be a magnet. This chapter is about why it is one, and why hydrogen in particular.

1.1  The one property that makes MRI possible

A proton has three properties that matter here: it has charge, it has mass, and it has spin — an intrinsic angular momentum that it carries the way it carries its charge, as an unchangeable fact about what it is. Spin is not rotation. Nothing is going around. A point particle with no measurable radius still has spin ½ ℏ, which rules out any picture of a small ball turning. But the consequences of spin are exactly the consequences you would get from a spinning charged ball, and that is why the classical picture survives in every textbook including this one.

The consequence we care about: a particle with spin angular momentum J and charge has a magnetic dipole moment μ pointing along the same axis, proportional to it.

1.1 μ = γ J The constant γ is the gyromagnetic ratio — a fixed number for each nuclear species, measured in rad·s⁻¹·T⁻¹, and almost always quoted in the divided form γ̄ = γ/2π in MHz per tesla. It is the single number that converts magnetic field into frequency, and it will follow you through this entire guide.

For a proton, γ/2π = 42.577 MHz/T. At 1.5 T that is 63.87 MHz; at 3 T, 127.7 MHz. Those are FM-radio and VHF-television frequencies, which is not a coincidence you should ignore — it dictates the size of the antennas, the shielding of the room, and the fact that the whole scanner sits inside a copper box.

The trap to avoid early

Almost every confusion in early MRI study comes from mixing picture (c) with picture (a). The two-state quantum picture is correct for a single nucleus and is genuinely needed to explain relaxation and chemical shift. But MRI never measures a single nucleus — it measures the sum over roughly 1021 of them per voxel, and that sum behaves exactly like one classical vector obeying classical equations. Use (c) to reason about energy and populations; use (a) to reason about anything that moves. Chapter 2 makes that transition explicit.

1.2  Which nuclei can do this at all

Not every nucleus has spin. Protons and neutrons pair off inside the nucleus with opposite spins, so a nucleus with an even number of both has zero net spin, zero magnetic moment, and is completely invisible to MRI. That immediately eliminates the two most abundant elements in tissue after hydrogen: 12C and 16O.

Nuclear spin follows from parity of the proton and neutron counts. Odd mass number → half-integer spin; odd–odd → integer spin; even–even → no spin at all.

Among the visible nuclei, hydrogen wins by an enormous margin, and it wins for three separate reasons that multiply together.

The three reasons hydrogen wins

  1. Abundance. Soft tissue is 60–80% water by mass, and every water molecule carries two hydrogen nuclei. Fat adds many more. A voxel of brain contains on the order of 1021 protons — the statistics are so good that a signal from a one-in-a-million population imbalance is still measurable.
  2. The largest useful γ. 42.58 MHz/T, versus 11.26 for sodium. Higher γ means a bigger energy split (more polarisation), a bigger moment, and a faster-changing flux in the receive coil. All three help.
  3. Spin ½. Nuclei with I > ½ have an electric quadrupole moment that couples to local electric field gradients and relaxes them viciously fast — sodium's T2 has a component of a few milliseconds, so the signal is gone before most sequences can read it. Spin-½ nuclei have no quadrupole moment and stay coherent long enough to be imaged comfortably.

1.3  How much magnetism is actually there?

It is worth internalising how weak this effect is, because it explains every design decision in the rest of the guide — the superconducting magnet, the shielded room, the long scan times, the obsession with SNR.

The nuclear magneton is about 5.05 × 10−27 J/T. The proton's moment is about 1.41 × 10−26 J/T — roughly 1/658 of the electron's magnetic moment, which is itself microscopic. In a 1.5 T field at body temperature, the energy difference between the two proton states is around 10−7 eV, while the ambient thermal energy kT is about 2.6 × 10−2 eV. Thermal agitation beats the magnetic ordering by a factor of ~105.

The number that should shock you

At 1.5 T and 37 °C, only about 5 protons in every million constitute the excess in the lower energy state. Every MR image you have ever seen was made from that fraction; the other 999 995 cancel exactly. Chapter 2 derives this number, and it is the reason field strength matters so much commercially — the excess scales linearly with B₀.

1.4  Where the hydrogen actually is

MRI does not see “tissue”; it sees mobile hydrogen. That qualifier does real work. Protons locked into a rigid crystal lattice — cortical bone, tooth enamel, tendon collagen — have T2 values on the order of tens of microseconds, so their signal decays before a conventional sequence can even switch its receiver on. That is why bone is black on MRI and brilliant on CT: not because the hydrogen is absent, but because it dephases instantly. Ultrashort-echo-time (UTE) and zero-echo-time (ZTE) sequences exist precisely to catch it (Chapter 11).

1.5  Chemical shift: the nucleus does not feel the field you applied

One refinement that becomes a whole imaging problem later. The nucleus sits inside an electron cloud, and those electrons circulate in response to B₀, generating a small opposing field. The nucleus therefore experiences Blocal = B₀(1 − σ), where σ is the shielding constant, on the order of parts per million and dependent on the chemical bond.

1.2 flocal = (γ/2π) · B₀ · (1 − σ) Protons in the −CH₂− chains of triglycerides resonate about 3.5 ppm lower than protons in water. At 1.5 T that is 3.5 × 63.87 ≈ 220 Hz; at 3 T it doubles to ~440 Hz.

That 3.5 ppm gap is responsible for: the black-and-white edge line at every fat–water boundary (chemical shift artifact of the first kind, Chapter 15), all fat-suppression techniques (Chapter 11), Dixon water–fat separation, and the entire field of MR spectroscopy (Chapter 13). A parts-per-million effect becomes a first-order clinical concern because we measure frequency with astonishing precision.

Key points

  • μ = γJ. Spin is intrinsic angular momentum; the magnetic moment is proportional to it, and γ is the conversion constant that runs through the entire subject.
  • γ/2π for ¹H is 42.58 MHz/T — memorise it. 1.5 T → 63.9 MHz, 3 T → 127.7 MHz.
  • Even–even nuclei are invisible. Hydrogen dominates clinical MRI by abundance, by γ, and by being spin-½ (no quadrupolar broadening).
  • The effect is astonishingly weak — a few parts per million of the spins contribute — which is why the machine is built the way it is.
  • MRI sees mobile protons only. Rigidly bound hydrogen dephases in microseconds and is effectively absent.
  • Electron shielding shifts frequencies by ppm, and that ppm is the basis of fat suppression, chemical shift artifact and spectroscopy.
