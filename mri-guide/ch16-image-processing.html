The Image Processing Pipeline: DICOM to Analysis

This is where the physics hands over. If you are going to build algorithms on MRI, the preceding fifteen chapters are not background reading — they are the list of reasons your pixel values mean something different from what you assumed. This chapter is the handover document.

16.1  What comes off the scanner

Clinical output is DICOM: one file per slice (or a multi-frame object), each carrying a large header of tagged metadata alongside the pixel data. The header is the interesting part, and it is where most downstream bugs are born.

TagNameWhy it matters
(0020,0037)ImageOrientationPatientTwo direction cosine vectors for the row and column axes. With the next tag it defines the slice's position in world space. Get this wrong and left/right flips — a genuine clinical hazard
(0020,0032)ImagePositionPatientWorld coordinates of the first voxel. Sorting slices by this, not by instance number, is the only reliable way to build a volume
(0028,0030)PixelSpacingIn-plane mm. Note it is row spacing then column spacing — the opposite order from what most people assume
(0018,0050) / (0018,0088)SliceThickness / SpacingBetweenSlicesThese are not the same number when there is a slice gap. Using thickness as spacing silently squashes the volume
(0028,1052) / (0028,1053)RescaleIntercept / RescaleSlopeStored values must be transformed before use. Ignoring them is a common and silent error
(0028,1050) / (0028,1051)WindowCenter / WindowWidthDisplay preference only. Never bake window settings into training data
(0018,0080) / (0018,0081) / (0018,1314)RepetitionTime / EchoTime / FlipAngleYour record of the physics. Essential for reasoning about contrast — and for spotting that "T2" in the series description is actually FLAIR
(0008,103E)SeriesDescriptionFree text set by whoever built the protocol. Inconsistent, often wrong, and yet almost every pipeline routes on it. Verify against the acquisition parameters
The practical route

For research, convert once with a well-tested tool — dcm2niix is the de-facto standard — into NIfTI, which stores a single volume plus an affine matrix mapping voxel indices to world millimetres. Organise with the BIDS convention so that every later tool knows where things are. Keep the DICOM headers: sidecar JSON from dcm2niix preserves TR, TE, flip angle, coil, and vendor-specific fields that you will eventually need. And be aware that DICOM de-identification is not a matter of blanking a few tags — burned-in pixel annotations, private vendor tags and the face itself (a 1 mm isotropic T1 volume renders a recognisable face) all require handling.

16.2  The central fact: MRI intensities are not quantitative

A CT number is Hounsfield units — physically defined, comparable across scanners and decades. An MRI intensity is an arbitrary number produced by a signal equation you do not fully know, multiplied by coil sensitivity, scaled by a receiver gain set automatically for that patient, and rescaled again for display. The same white matter can be 300 in one scan and 1400 in the next on the same machine an hour later.

16.3  Geometry, and the affine that everyone gets wrong once

A volume is not an array; it is an array plus a mapping into physical space. That mapping is a 4×4 affine matrix carrying voxel size, orientation and origin. Two conventions dominate and they disagree about signs: DICOM/ITK use LPS (+x left, +y posterior, +z superior) while NIfTI and most neuroimaging tools use RAS. Converting between them flips two axes. Every left-right mix-up in the literature comes from this.

  • Resample deliberately. Registration, atlas mapping and network input sizes all imply resampling. Use linear or B-spline interpolation for images, and nearest neighbour for label maps — interpolating labels invents nonexistent classes at boundaries.
  • Anisotropic voxels are the norm. A clinical 2D T2 is often 0.5 × 0.5 × 5 mm. Feeding that to an isotropic 3D network without resampling means the network sees a ten-to-one geometric distortion it was never told about.
  • Distortion is real geometry, not noise. EPI-based diffusion and fMRI are warped by several millimetres near air; correcting with a field map or reverse-phase-encode pair (topup) is mandatory before overlaying them on an anatomical scan.

16.5  Machine learning on MRI: the failure modes that are specific to this modality

Failure modeWhat happensMitigation
The model learns the scannerSite and vendor are correlated with the label in most multi-centre datasets. A classifier can reach high accuracy purely from noise texture and recon filteringSite-stratified splits, leave-one-site-out validation, ComBat, aggressive intensity augmentation, and testing on a genuinely external site
Patient leakageSlices or timepoints from the same patient land in both train and test; performance is inflated dramaticallySplit by patient, always. Never by slice, never by study
Contrast is not standardised"T1-weighted" spans a wide range of TR/TE/sequence; a model trained on MPRAGE may fail on 2D spin-echo T1Filter by acquisition parameters not series description; augment across contrast; or train on physics-simulated variation
Anisotropic and inconsistent geometryNetworks assume consistent voxel size; clinical data does not provide itResample to a fixed spacing, or use spacing-aware architectures (nnU-Net does this automatically and is a strong baseline for exactly this reason)
Class imbalanceA lesion may be 0.01% of voxels; a network that predicts all-background scores 99.99% accuracyDice or Tversky losses, patch sampling biased to the foreground, and reporting sensitivity per lesion, not per voxel
Metric–task mismatchHigh Dice on large structures hides complete failure on the small ones that matterReport lesion-wise detection, size-stratified Dice, and surface distances alongside volumetric overlap
Artifacts as confoundersMotion correlates with pathology (patients who are unwell move more), so a model can learn motion instead of diseaseQuality control every input; record and adjust for QC metrics; exclude on prospective criteria, not on outcome
Reconstruction hallucinationAccelerated learned reconstruction may synthesise or erase small features upstream of your modelKnow which recon produced your data; prefer classically reconstructed data for quantitative work
The most useful habit you can build

For every dataset, write down the acquisition parameters of every series you use: field strength, vendor, sequence, TR, TE, TI, flip angle, voxel size, acceleration factor, reconstruction method. Not because you will use them all as features, but because when the model behaves strangely, that table is where the answer almost always is. Half of "the model does not generalise" turns out on inspection to be "site B used a different sequence".

16.7  A checklist before you train anything

  1. Do I know the sequence and parameters of every series, from the headers rather than the description?
  2. Have I looked at fifty images, including the bad ones? Do I know what the artifacts in this dataset look like?
  3. Is my split by patient, and stratified by site and scanner?
  4. Have I applied bias correction and a defensible intensity normalisation, in that order?
  5. Is the geometry consistent — spacing, orientation, and the affine — and have I verified an overlay visually?
  6. Are labels interpolated with nearest neighbour?
  7. Does my metric reflect the clinical task, including small and rare targets?
  8. Do I have an external test set from a scanner the model has never seen?
  9. Can I explain what a failure case has in common, in physics terms?

Key points

  • DICOM headers carry the geometry and the physics. Sort by position, respect rescale slope, distrust the series description.
  • MRI intensities are arbitrary. Bias correction and normalisation are not optional preprocessing niceties; they are what makes the data comparable at all.
  • Bias correction precedes segmentation, because a smooth multiplicative field defeats any intensity threshold.
  • Geometry is a matrix, not a shape. LPS versus RAS causes real left–right errors.
  • The commonest ML failure is learning the scanner, and the fix is validation design, not architecture.
  • Everything upstream is visible downstream — which is precisely why the previous fifteen chapters were worth reading.
