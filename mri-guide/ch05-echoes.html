Echoes: Recovering Phase You Thought You Had Lost

Erwin Hahn discovered in 1950 that dephased spins can be brought back into step, producing a signal out of what looked like noise. That discovery is why MRI exists in its present form: essentially every clinical sequence measures an echo, never a raw FID.

5.1  The problem an echo solves

Chapter 4 left us with an awkward situation. The free induction decay dies with T2*, which is dominated by static field inhomogeneity — imperfect shim, air–tissue interfaces, the patient's own susceptibility variations. That is equipment and anatomy, not tissue biology. If we measured only FIDs, our images would be maps of how badly the magnet was shimmed.

The saving grace is that static dephasing is deterministic. A spin sitting in a field 0.1 μT above nominal accumulates phase at a constant excess rate. It is not random; it is just ahead. And anything that is merely ahead can be made to be behind.

The runners analogy — the one worth memorising

Fire a starting gun and a hundred runners set off around a track at different speeds. After 30 seconds they are spread all around the loop: the crowd's “centre of mass” is at the middle of the track, i.e. zero net signal. Now blow a whistle and order everyone to turn around and run back at the same speed. After another 30 seconds every single runner — fast or slow — arrives back at the start line simultaneously. That instant of re-gathering is the echo. The whistle is the 180° pulse; the time from gun to whistle is TE/2.

What the trick cannot fix: runners who randomly change speed. Those never re-gather, and their loss is exactly T2. This is why a spin echo restores T2* losses but not T2 losses.

5.2  The spin echo

The 180° refocusing pulse rotates the whole transverse plane about an axis in it (conventionally x′ or y′). A spin that had accumulated phase +φ finds itself at −φ, while its rate of phase accumulation is unchanged, because it is still sitting in the same slightly-wrong field. So it keeps accumulating +φ per unit time from a starting point of −φ, and reaches zero exactly one interval later.

5.1 TE = 2τ     S(TE) = M₀ e−TE/T2  — the echo peak follows the true T2 envelope τ is the 90°→180° interval. The echo forms at the same distance after the refocusing pulse as the excitation was before it. Play with the timing and the echo moves with it; this is the knob called TE, and Chapter 12 is about what you buy with it.

5.3  The gradient echo, and why it is a different animal

You can also dephase spins deliberately, using an imaging gradient, and then rephase them by reversing that gradient's polarity. The area under the gradient waveform is the phase it imparts; make the negative area equal the positive area and the phase cancels. The echo forms at the moment of equal areas.

5.4  Echo trains and the CPMG condition

One 180° pulse gives one echo. Apply a train of them and you get an echo after each, with peaks tracing out the T2 decay curve — the Carr–Purcell experiment. Meiboom and Gill added a crucial refinement: shift the phase of the refocusing pulses by 90° relative to the excitation. Then any small error in the 180° flip angle alternates sign from echo to echo instead of accumulating, and a train of hundreds of echoes remains stable. This is the CPMG condition, and it is the reason turbo/fast spin echo (Chapter 11) is practical at all.

5.5  Stimulated echoes and the wider family

Three pulses of any flip angle produce not one echo but five. Two 90° pulses form a Hahn echo; the third pulse revives magnetization that was “parked” along z between pulses two and three, producing a stimulated echo. That parked magnetization is protected from T2 while it waits — it decays with T1 instead — which is exploited deliberately by STEAM spectroscopy and by diffusion sequences that need long mixing times.

Extended phase graphs

Once a sequence has many RF pulses, the bookkeeping of which coherence pathway produces which echo becomes unmanageable by hand. The extended phase graph (EPG) formalism tracks magnetization as a set of configuration states indexed by accumulated dephasing order, and each RF pulse mixes them by a fixed matrix. It is the standard tool for predicting the signal in TSE trains with imperfect refocusing, for bSSFP, and for T2 mapping from multi-echo data. If you go on to sequence development, EPG is the thing to learn after the Bloch equations.

Key points

  • Static dephasing is reversible; random dephasing is not. That single distinction defines T2* versus T2.
  • A 180° pulse mirrors phase, so all constant-rate spins re-gather at TE = 2τ.
  • Spin echo peaks follow T2; gradient echo peaks follow T2*. Choose accordingly.
  • Gradient echoes are fast and susceptibility-sensitive; spin echoes are clean and SAR-hungry.
  • CPMG phase cycling makes long echo trains robust, enabling TSE and all fast T2 imaging.
  • Stimulated echoes store magnetization along z where only T1 acts on it.
