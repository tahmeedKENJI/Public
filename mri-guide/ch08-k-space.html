k-Space: The Domain the Scanner Actually Measures

An MRI scanner does not acquire an image and it does not acquire pixels. It acquires Fourier coefficients — one complex number per sample, each describing how much of a particular striped pattern is present in the object. k-space is the ledger of those coefficients, and learning to read it is the difference between memorising MRI and understanding it.

8.1  The definition, and why it is inevitable

From Chapter 7, with a gradient applied the received signal is

8.1 s(t) = ∫ ρ(r) e−i2π k(t)·r dr      k(t) = (γ/2π) ∫0t G(t′) dt′ Read the second equation carefully, because it is the operating manual for the entire scanner: the gradient is the velocity with which you move through k-space, and the position you reach is the accumulated area under the gradient waveform. Turn a gradient on and you travel; turn it off and you stop where you are; reverse it and you travel back.

Each sample of s(t) is therefore the value of the object's Fourier transform at one point k. Collect enough points on a grid, run a 2D inverse FFT, and you have ρ(r). That is the whole of conventional MRI reconstruction.

8.2  One point of k-space is one stripe pattern

The most common misconception is that the centre of k-space corresponds to the centre of the image. It does not. Every point in k-space contains information about every point in the image. A single k-space sample is the amplitude and phase of one sinusoidal grating spanning the entire field of view: its spatial frequency is |k| and its orientation is the direction of k.

8.4  Trajectories: gradients are the steering wheel

Cartesian line-by-line filling is the default, but nothing in the physics requires it. Any path through k-space is legal as long as you can compute where you are, and different paths trade speed, artifact behaviour and hardware demand against each other.

8.5  Sampling rules, stated once and used forever

8.2 · 8.3 FOV = 1 / Δk       Δx = 1 / (2 kmax) = FOV / N Both are Nyquist statements. Sampling k too sparsely (Δk too large) shrinks the FOV below the object and the object folds back on itself. Truncating k too early (kmax too small) blurs, and — because truncation is a rectangular window whose transform is a sinc — it also rings.
A diagnostic habit worth building

When you see an unfamiliar artifact, ask: what would k-space have to look like to produce this? Ghosts repeating at FOV/2 → something alternated between even and odd lines. A single herringbone → one corrupted sample. Ringing at sharp edges → k-space was truncated. Blurring in one direction only → that axis lost its high frequencies. Half the image folded over the other → Δk was too large. This question converts artifact identification from memorisation into deduction.

Partial Fourier and conjugate symmetry

If the object were perfectly real-valued, its Fourier transform would satisfy S(−k) = S*(k): each sample would be the complex conjugate of the one opposite it through the origin. You could acquire half of k-space and infer the rest, halving scan time. In reality the image is not real — B₀ inhomogeneity, flow, and coil phase all impose slowly-varying phase — so practical implementations acquire slightly more than half (typically 60–75%), use the extra central lines to estimate that phase, and then synthesise the missing region. The cost is √(fraction) SNR and some sensitivity to phase errors near sharp field changes.

Key points

  • k(t) = (γ/2π)∫G dt. Gradients are velocity in k-space; the acquisition is a path, not a picture.
  • Every k sample encodes the whole image as one grating. There is no local correspondence.
  • Centre = contrast; periphery = detail. Acquisition order therefore controls contrast.
  • FOV = 1/Δk and Δx = 1/(2kmax) — the two Nyquist relations behind nearly every artifact.
  • Any trajectory is legal; Cartesian, EPI, radial and spiral trade speed against artifact character.
  • Hermitian symmetry allows partial Fourier, limited in practice by real image phase.
