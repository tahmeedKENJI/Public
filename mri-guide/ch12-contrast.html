Part IV · Sequences and Contrast

Contrast: TR, TE, TI and Why Tissues Look Different

This is the chapter where the physics becomes radiology. Two tissues with identical proton density can be made to differ by a factor of five, or to look identical, purely by choosing two numbers. The simulator below computes real signal from real relaxation times, so you can turn those numbers and watch an image change weighting in front of you.

12.1  The signal equation, and what each factor does

12.1 S = k · ρ · (1 − e−TR/T1) · e−TE/T2 Spin echo, 90° excitation, long enough TR that the steady state is simple. Three factors, three sources of contrast, and they multiply. The whole art is arranging for exactly one of them to dominate — mixed weighting is the commonest protocol error, because the two effects can cancel and make genuinely different tissues look the same.

The rule that makes the table unnecessary

Short TR makes it T1-weighted. Long TE makes it T2-weighted. Long TR and short TE leave only proton density. You cannot get both T1 and T2 weighting at once — short TR plus long TE gives a mixed, low-signal image in which the two contrasts partially cancel, which is why that quadrant of the parameter space is simply not used.

How to identify the weighting of an image you are handed

  1. Find CSF. Bright ventricles → T2-weighted. Dark ventricles → T1-weighted or FLAIR. Mid-grey → proton density.
  2. If CSF is dark, check the grey–white contrast. On T1 white matter is brighter than grey (myelin lipid shortens T1). If CSF is dark but white matter lesions are bright, it is FLAIR.
  3. Check fat. Subcutaneous fat dark → some fat suppression is running; on STIR everything with a short T1 is dark.
  4. Check for flow voids and susceptibility. Prominent blooming around vessels and haemorrhage, plus visible venous structure, suggests a T2*-weighted gradient echo.

12.5  Contrast is not the same as contrast-to-noise

12.2 CNRAB = (SA − SB) / σnoise = SNRA − SNRB A parameter choice that maximises the difference may do so at the cost of so much signal that the difference disappears into noise. This is why very long TE is not automatically better for T2 lesion detection: past roughly TE ≈ T2 of the lesion, contrast rises while both signals fall, and CNR turns over. As a rule of thumb the optimum TE for detecting a lesion against background is near the T2 of the background.

12.6  What field strength does to contrast

  • T1 lengthens with B₀ (Chapter 4's BPP curve), and lengthens by different amounts in different tissues. Grey–white T1 contrast is therefore worse at 3 T for the same TR; protocols compensate with longer TR/TI or by using inversion preparation.
  • T2 changes little; T2* shortens markedly. Susceptibility contrast — SWI, BOLD, iron-sensitive imaging — improves substantially at higher field. This is the main reason fMRI migrated to 3 T and 7 T.
  • Chemical shift doubles in Hz from 1.5 T to 3 T, which makes spectral fat suppression easier and chemical-shift misregistration worse.
  • SAR quadruples, constraining the very TSE sequences that provide T2 contrast.

12.7  Contrast agents in practice

Gadolinium shortens T1 wherever it reaches, so enhancement is seen on T1-weighted images acquired after injection — always compared against a pre-contrast T1 acquired with identical parameters. Enhancement means the agent got there, which means either normal vascularity or a breakdown of a barrier that normally excludes it. In the brain, enhancement is essentially a map of blood–brain-barrier integrity: high-grade tumour, active demyelination, infection, infarct after a few days, and meninges all enhance; normal brain does not.

Two traps worth remembering

Never run STIR after gadolinium. Enhancing tissue has a shortened T1, close to fat's, so STIR will null the very thing you injected contrast to see. Use spectral fat saturation instead.

Very high gadolinium concentration causes signal loss, not gain. The T2* shortening overwhelms the T1 shortening — visible in the bladder late in an exam, and in the arterial input function of perfusion studies, where it is a genuine quantification problem.

Key points

  • S = ρ(1−e−TR/T1)e−TE/T2. Three multiplicative factors; arrange for one to dominate.
  • Short TR → T1 weighting. Long TE → T2 weighting. Long TR + short TE → proton density.
  • The fourth quadrant is empty because T1 and T2 effects oppose each other there.
  • TI nulls a tissue at 0.693 × T1; that is the entire difference between STIR and FLAIR.
  • Optimise CNR, not contrast — the difference divided by the noise is what determines detection.
  • Contrast changes with field strength, so protocols do not port between scanners unchanged.
