Resonance: RF Excitation and the Rotating Frame

A 3 tesla magnet holds the magnetization along z. A transmit coil produces a field of maybe 20 microtesla — 150 000 times weaker — and yet it can tip that magnetization all the way into the transverse plane in under a millisecond. The reason is resonance, and the tool that makes it obvious is a change of coordinates.

3.1  The problem with the lab frame

In the laboratory frame, M is precessing about B₀ at 127 MHz. If you apply a small static field along x, it does essentially nothing: the magnetization sweeps past it 127 million times a second, and the tiny torque it feels reverses direction every half turn, averaging to zero. A constant push cannot move something that is spinning that fast.

But if the small field rotates with the magnetization — if it stays on the same side of it, turn after turn — then its tiny torque accumulates instead of cancelling. That is what a resonant RF pulse is: a magnetic field, called B₁, rotating in the transverse plane at exactly the Larmor frequency.

The playground-swing analogy, used properly

Pushing a child on a swing works not because the push is strong but because it is synchronised. Push at the natural frequency and small pushes accumulate into a large amplitude. Push at any other frequency and the pushes fight each other. B₁ is the push; ω₀ is the natural frequency; the flip angle is the amplitude you build up. The analogy also gets the failure mode right — detune slightly and the excitation becomes partial and phase-shifted, which is precisely the off-resonance behaviour explored below.

3.2  The rotating frame

Instead of tracking a vector that turns 127 million times a second, sit on a turntable that rotates about z at the RF frequency ωrf. In this frame the fast precession disappears. What remains is an effective field:

3.1 Beff = (B₀ − ωrf/γ) ẑ + B₁ x̂′ The rotation subtracts a fictitious field ωrf/γ from the z axis — exactly the same mathematical move as the centrifugal term in a rotating mechanical frame. On resonance, ωrf = γB₀, the z term cancels completely, and the only field left in the universe of this frame is the microtesla B₁ lying along x′.

That cancellation is the whole trick. In the rotating frame, a 3 T magnet is gone, and the magnetization simply precesses about B₁ at the modest rate ω₁ = γB₁ — a few kilohertz. Precessing about an axis in the transverse plane means tipping away from z. The angle swept is:

3.2 θ = γ B₁ τ   (rectangular pulse)   or   θ = γ ∫₀τ B₁(t) dt  (any shape) The flip angle is the area under the B₁ envelope, nothing more. A 90° pulse needs γB₁τ = π/2. With B₁ = 20 μT, ω₁/2π = 851 Hz, so τ ≈ 294 μs. Halve B₁ and you double the duration for the same flip — the trade that governs SAR, slice profile and pulse design forever after.

3.3  Off resonance, and the tilted effective field

When ωrf ≠ γB₀, the z-component does not cancel. Writing Δω = ω₀ − ωrf, the effective field in the rotating frame has magnitude and tilt:

3.3 ωeff = √(ω₁² + Δω²)     tan α = ω₁ / Δω M precesses about that tilted axis. The maximum angle it can reach from z is 2α, so far off resonance (Δω ≫ ω₁) the spins barely move at all — they are simply not excited. The transition from “fully excited” to “untouched” happens over a bandwidth of roughly ω₁, which is why a stronger, shorter pulse excites a wider band of frequencies.

3.4  Pulse shapes: the Fourier view of excitation

To a very good approximation (valid for small flip angles, and used well beyond that in practice), the excitation profile in frequency is the Fourier transform of the B₁ envelope in time. This is the single most useful design rule in RF pulse engineering.

3.5  Adiabatic pulses: sweeping the field instead of hitting it

Ordinary pulses depend on B₁ being the value you assumed — if the transmit coil is 30% weaker at the edge of its reach, you get a 63° pulse where you wanted 90°. Adiabatic pulses escape this entirely with a beautiful idea: sweep the RF frequency from far below resonance to far above it while ramping B₁. In the rotating frame, Beff starts pointing along +z, tips through the transverse plane, and ends pointing along −z. If the sweep is slow enough that M can keep up — the adiabatic condition, |dα/dt| ≪ ωeff — then M is dragged along with Beff and ends up inverted, regardless of exactly how strong B₁ was, as long as it exceeded a threshold.

Where you meet this in the wild

Adiabatic inversion (hyperbolic secant) is the default for inversion-recovery prep on 3 T and above, for arterial spin labelling (Chapter 13), and for anything using a surface transmit coil whose B₁ falls off dramatically with depth. The price is SAR: an adiabatic pulse can deposit an order of magnitude more energy than a conventional inversion.

3.6  What the pulse costs: a first look at SAR

B₁ is delivered as an electromagnetic wave into a conductive, salty body. Some of it induces eddy currents and dissipates as heat. Specific Absorption Rate, in W/kg, scales as:

3.4 SAR ∝ σ B₀² B₁² · D   ∝   θ² · BW · D / τ σ is tissue conductivity and D the duty cycle. The B₀² term is the reason 3 T scanners spend so much of their life waiting: the same TSE train that is comfortable at 1.5 T is four times hotter at 3 T. Practical responses: lower refocusing flip angles (Chapter 11), longer TR, fewer slices per TR, or hyperecho/variable-flip trains.

Key points

  • Resonance is synchronisation. B₁ works because it rotates with M, not because it is strong.
  • The rotating frame cancels B₀ exactly on resonance, leaving only B₁ — this is why a microtesla field beats a tesla field.
  • Flip angle = γ × area under the B₁ envelope. Amplitude and duration trade off freely.
  • Off resonance, Beff tilts, the achievable flip collapses, and selectivity is born.
  • Excitation profile ≈ Fourier transform of the pulse envelope — sinc in time gives a rectangle in frequency.
  • Adiabatic pulses trade energy for immunity to B₁ error.
  • SAR ∝ B₀²θ², which is why high-field protocols are constrained by heating, not by physics.
