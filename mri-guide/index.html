From nuclear spin to image processing

Magnetic Resonance Imaging
A Visual Study Guide

Sixteen chapters covering the whole chain: the quantum origin of the signal, the machine that produces it, the mathematics that turns it into a picture, and the pixels that come out the other end. Written for someone who has read the equations and wants to see what they mean — every chapter carries figures and interactive simulations that compute the real physics rather than illustrate it.

Compiled by Tahmeed and Claude · for personal study

How to use this

The chapters build on each other and are best read in order the first time — Chapter 8 assumes Chapter 7, and everything after Chapter 10 assumes Chapter 8. Afterwards they stand alone as reference.

Play with the widgets. They are not decorations. The k-space editor in Chapter 8, the contrast simulator in Chapter 12 and the SENSE reconstruction in Chapter 14 run genuine algorithms — real Fourier transforms, real signal equations, a real least-squares unfolding — on a synthetic phantom built from published tissue relaxation times. Dragging a slider until something breaks teaches more than a paragraph does.

Everything works offline. No fonts, scripts or images are fetched from the network; the figures are drawn in the page. External links are provided for going deeper, and video links are deliberately search queries rather than specific video IDs so that they do not rot.

Standing resources

The one to bookmarkQuestions and Answers in MRI — Allen D. Elster Several hundred short pages, each answering one specific question, written by a radiologist who understands the physics properly. Free. If you read only one other source, read this one. CasesRadiopaedia Where to build visual vocabulary: thousands of labelled cases with the sequence stated, plus solid physics articles. CommunityISMRM The field’s society. Educational sessions, weekly online seminars and study-group material — much of it free and considerably deeper than textbook level. Hands-onBART Reconstruct real raw data yourself: SENSE, GRAPPA, ESPIRiT, compressed sensing. Pairs naturally with the fastMRI datasets. DatafastMRI Open raw k-space and image datasets. The fastest way to move from reading about k-space to holding some. Hands-onPulseq Write a pulse sequence in a vendor-neutral format and run it on a real scanner. The natural next step after Chapter 11.

Books, in the order they become useful

  1. McRobbie, Moore, Graves, Prince — MRI from Picture to Proton (CUP). Starts from the image and works backwards. The friendliest entry point, and unusually good on why things are done the way they are.
  2. Nishimura — Principles of Magnetic Resonance Imaging (Stanford). Short, dense, signal-processing-flavoured. The best value per page if you are comfortable with Fourier analysis.
  3. Haacke, Brown, Thompson, Venkatesan — Magnetic Resonance Imaging: Physical Principles and Sequence Design (Wiley). The standard graduate text; complete derivations.
  4. Bernstein, King, Zhou — Handbook of MRI Pulse Sequences (Elsevier). Not a textbook but an encyclopaedia: every pulse, gradient waveform and sequence, with implementation detail. Indispensable if you go near sequence development.
  5. Levitt — Spin Dynamics (Wiley). For when the quantum description stops being optional — NMR-oriented, superbly written.
  6. Shellock — Reference Manual for Magnetic Resonance Safety, Implants and Devices. The safety authority, updated annually.
On the figures and the numbers

The phantom used throughout is synthetic — ellipses assigned published relaxation times, not real patient data. Signal values are computed from the standard signal equations, so the images respond correctly to parameter changes, but they are a physics demonstration and nothing more. Relaxation times vary by 10–20% between published studies; the values here follow Stanisz et al. (2005) and Wansapura et al. (1999) and are appropriate for reasoning about sequence design, not for quantitation. Nothing in this guide is clinical advice.
