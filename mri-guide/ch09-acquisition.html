Part III · Making an Image

Signal Acquisition: Coils, Demodulation, Sampling, SNR

The magnetization is rotating. Now it has to become numbers. This chapter follows the signal from a few microvolts induced in a loop of copper, through a mixer that strips off a 127 MHz carrier, into an analogue-to-digital converter — and quantifies exactly what you sacrifice at each protocol decision along the way.

9.1  Detection is Faraday's law, nothing more

A loop of wire near precessing transverse magnetization experiences a changing magnetic flux, and a changing flux induces an EMF. That is the entire detection principle — the same law that runs a bicycle dynamo.

9.1 emf = − dΦ/dt   ∝   ω0 · Mxy · B1−/i The factor ω₀ is why signal grows with field strength twice: once through M₀ ∝ B₀ (Chapter 2) and once through the rate of change here. The last factor is the principle of reciprocity: a coil's receive sensitivity at a point equals the B₁ field it would produce at that point per unit current. This is enormously convenient — you can compute where a coil "sees" by simulating what it would transmit.

Signal levels are on the order of microvolts. Thermal noise from the patient's own conductive tissue is comparable, and everything else in the universe — FM radio, LED drivers, the lift motor — is larger. Hence the Faraday cage, the preamplifier mounted at the coil, and the obsessive attention to grounding.

9.2  Demodulation and quadrature detection

The raw signal oscillates at 64 or 128 MHz, but the information occupies only a few hundred kilohertz around that. Digitising the carrier directly would be wasteful, so the receiver mixes the signal down to baseband: multiply by a reference at f₀ and low-pass filter, leaving only the difference frequencies — precisely the offsets caused by gradients and chemical shift.

Multiplying by a single reference, however, loses the sign of the offset: a spin 500 Hz above resonance and one 500 Hz below produce identical outputs. The fix is quadrature detection — mix with both cos(ω₀t) and sin(ω₀t), producing two channels, conventionally called I (in-phase) and Q (quadrature). Together they form a complex signal whose phase rotates in a direction that distinguishes positive from negative offsets.

9.3  Bandwidth, dwell time, and what "bandwidth" means on the console

9.2 Δt = 1 / BWtotal     Treadout = Nx · Δt     BWpixel = BWtotal / Nx Vendors quote this differently and it causes endless confusion: some report total bandwidth in kHz (e.g. ±32 kHz), others report bandwidth per pixel in Hz/px. They describe the same setting. 64 kHz total over a 256 matrix is 250 Hz/px, a dwell time of 15.6 μs and a readout lasting 4 ms.

9.4  Signal-to-noise ratio: the master equation

Noise in MRI is dominated by thermal (Johnson) noise from the conductive tissue in the coil's sensitive volume, not by the electronics. It is white across the receive bandwidth. Signal comes from the number of spins in a voxel. Putting those together, with all constants absorbed:

9.3 SNR ∝ (Δx · Δy · Δz) · √( NPE · Nfreq · NEX / BW ) · f(B₀, coil, sequence) Read it as: SNR is proportional to voxel volume — the harshest term, since halving each dimension costs a factor of eight — and to the square root of total acquisition time. Everything else is coil design, field strength and how much of the magnetization the sequence manages to use.

9.5  Coil arrays and how their signals are combined

A small surface coil has excellent SNR near it and none at depth. A large coil has uniform but poor SNR. The phased array resolves the dilemma: many small elements, each with its own receive chain, combined afterwards. Two standard combinations:

  • Root sum of squares — take |I1|² + |I2|² + …, then the square root. No knowledge of coil sensitivities needed, always works, but discards phase and adds a small positive bias in low-signal regions (a noise floor that never reaches zero).
  • Sensitivity-weighted (Roemer / adaptive) combination — weight each channel by its estimated complex sensitivity. Optimal SNR, preserves phase (needed for SWI and quantitative work), but requires a sensitivity estimate, usually from a fast pre-scan.
Why the number of channels stopped being a marketing number

Adding elements improves SNR only where the new element is close to the anatomy, and each element brings its own noise. Beyond a point you are paying for reconstruction burden, not image quality. The real benefit of many channels is parallel imaging: distinct spatial sensitivity profiles carry independent spatial information, which Chapter 14 converts directly into speed. A 32-channel head coil is bought for acceleration factor, not for raw SNR.

9.6  Practical details that bite

  • Oversampling in the readout direction is free. Sampling twice as fast doubles the FOV along x at no time cost (the readout is one continuous window), so vendors enable it by default and discard the extra afterwards. Aliasing along the readout direction should therefore never be seen on a modern scanner — but aliasing along the phase direction costs real time to prevent, so it is common.
  • NEX is not only for SNR. Averaging also suppresses incoherent motion artifacts, because ghosts add incoherently while signal adds coherently.
  • Partial (fractional) echo samples only part of the readout and reconstructs the rest, allowing shorter TE — useful for flow and short-T2* work.
  • Prescan is not optional. Before every acquisition the scanner sets the transmit gain (to calibrate B₁), the centre frequency (which drifts with gradient heating and with the patient's own susceptibility), and the receiver gain. A failed prescan is the cause of a surprising proportion of "the scanner is broken" reports.
  • Dynamic range matters. The DC sample at the centre of k-space is orders of magnitude larger than the peripheral samples; the ADC must accommodate the peak without clipping while still resolving the smallest. This is why receiver gain is set from a test acquisition, and why an incorrectly-gained scan produces bizarre, high-contrast garbage.

Key points

  • Detection is Faraday induction, and reciprocity means a coil receives where it would transmit.
  • Quadrature detection produces complex data — I and Q are physical channels, and MR data is inherently complex.
  • Δt = 1/BW; readout time = N·Δt. Bandwidth is a single knob trading SNR against distortion and TE.
  • SNR ∝ voxel volume × √(total sampling time). Resolution is expensive; time is only square-root effective.
  • Arrays give SNR near the surface and, more importantly, the spatial diversity that enables acceleration.
  • Noise comes mostly from the patient, which is why cooling the electronics helps less than you would expect.
