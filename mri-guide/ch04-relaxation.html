Relaxation: T1, T2, T2* and the Bloch Equations

Excitation is the same for every tissue. What makes a tumour look different from the brain around it is what happens afterwards — how fast the magnetization returns to equilibrium, and how fast the spins lose their common phase. These are two independent processes with two independent time constants, and essentially all of MRI contrast lives in the gap between them.

4.1  Two clocks, not one

Immediately after a 90° pulse, the magnetization lies in the transverse plane: Mz = 0, Mxy = M₀. The system is far from equilibrium in two separate ways, and it fixes them by two separate mechanisms.

4.2  Why T1 depends on molecular tumbling

T1 relaxation needs a mechanism to carry energy away, and the mechanism is other magnetic moments moving nearby. Each proton sits in the dipole field of its neighbours (Figure 1.1b); as molecules tumble, translate and vibrate, that local field fluctuates. Fourier-analyse the fluctuation and you get a spectral density J(ω): how much power the molecular motion has at each frequency. Only the power at the Larmor frequency can stimulate the transitions that change Mz.

4.3  T2 versus T2*: reversible and irreversible dephasing

The signal you actually observe after a single 90° pulse — the free induction decay, or FID — dies far faster than T2. Two different things are dephasing the spins at once:

  • True T2 — random, time-varying fields from neighbouring molecules. Each spin's accumulated phase error is a random walk. This is irreversible; no experiment can undo it.
  • T2′ — static field inhomogeneity: imperfect shimming, air–tissue interfaces, metal, deoxyhaemoglobin. A spin in a slightly higher field runs slightly fast, consistently. This is reversible, because a spin that has been running fast can be made to run slow.
4.3 1/T2* = 1/T2 + 1/T2′ = 1/T2 + γ ΔB0 Rates add, not times. Because T2′ is often much shorter than T2, T2* is dominated by inhomogeneity: in the brain at 3 T, T2 ≈ 80 ms but T2* ≈ 40 ms, and near the sinuses T2* can fall below 20 ms.

4.4  The Bloch equations

Felix Bloch wrote down the whole of classical MR in 1946 by adding two phenomenological decay terms to the precession equation. Every simulator, every sequence design tool, and every animation in this guide is ultimately integrating these three lines:

4.4 dM/dt = γ (M × B) − (Mxx̂ + Myŷ)/T2 − (Mz − M₀)ẑ/T1 First term: precession about whatever field is present (B₀, B₁, gradients — all of them at once). Second: transverse components leak away with time constant T2. Third: longitudinal component is pulled back toward M₀ with time constant T1. Nothing else is needed to describe a conventional imaging experiment.
What the Bloch equations deliberately omit

They are phenomenological — T1 and T2 are inserted as measured constants, not derived. They assume a single, well-mixed pool of spins, so they cannot describe magnetization transfer between free water and macromolecular protons, chemical exchange (CEST), or diffusion through a field gradient without extension (Bloch–Torrey adds a diffusion term; Bloch–McConnell adds exchanging pools). Each of those extensions became a whole imaging modality — see Chapter 13.

4.6  Changing relaxation on purpose: contrast agents

If relaxation is caused by fluctuating local magnetic fields, then adding a strong, tumbling magnetic moment to the water will accelerate it. That is exactly what a gadolinium chelate does. Gd³⁺ has seven unpaired electrons and a magnetic moment ~658 times larger than a proton's, so water molecules that transiently coordinate with it experience an enormous fluctuating field.

4.5 1/T1obs = 1/T10 + r1 · [Gd] Relaxivity r₁ is typically 3–5 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at 1.5 T. Relaxation rates add linearly in concentration — the basis of every quantitative perfusion and permeability model in Chapter 13. The same agent also shortens T2 (r₂ ≳ r₁), which is why very high local concentrations paradoxically cause signal loss.

Clinical caveat worth knowing

Gadolinium is toxic as a free ion; safety depends entirely on the chelate holding it. Linear chelates release more Gd than macrocyclic ones, are associated with nephrogenic systemic fibrosis in severe renal impairment, and are the reason several agents have been restricted or withdrawn in Europe. Gadolinium deposition in the dentate nucleus and globus pallidus is documented; its clinical significance remains unsettled. This is a real constraint on protocol design, not a footnote.

Key points

  • T1 and T2 are independent and describe different physics — energy loss versus phase loss. T2 ≤ T1 always.
  • T1 requires spectral power at ω₀; hence the BPP minimum, hence fat's short T1, hence T1 lengthening at high field.
  • T2* mixes true T2 with static inhomogeneity. 1/T2* = 1/T2 + γΔB₀; rates add.
  • The FID decays with T2*, not T2 — which is why almost every sequence forms an echo instead.
  • The Bloch equations are the complete classical description: precession plus two decay terms.
  • Contrast agents work by adding relaxation rate, linearly in concentration.
