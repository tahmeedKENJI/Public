From Signal to Image: Reconstruction and its Consequences

The core of MRI reconstruction is a single inverse FFT — a few milliseconds of arithmetic. Everything else in the pipeline exists because the data you actually collected is not quite what the FFT assumes: it is truncated, sampled on the wrong grid, phase-corrupted, spread across many coils, and incomplete. This chapter is about the gap between the ideal and the real.

10.1  The ideal case, in one line

Given a fully-sampled Cartesian k-space matrix S[kx, ky], the image is

10.1 ρ[x, y] = FFT−1{ S[kx, ky] } Complexity N²log N for an N×N image. On modern hardware a 256² slice reconstructs in well under a millisecond, which is why the interesting engineering has moved entirely to the cases where this formula does not directly apply.

10.2  The point spread function: what a single point becomes

An ideal imaging system would map a point source to a point. Ours maps it to the inverse Fourier transform of the k-space sampling window — the point spread function. Because we acquire a finite rectangular region of k-space, that PSF is a two-dimensional sinc: a central lobe whose width sets the true resolution, surrounded by oscillating side lobes that never quite vanish.

Those side lobes are visible at any sharp, high-contrast boundary as a series of parallel bright and dark bands — Gibbs ringing, also called truncation artifact. It is not noise, not a hardware fault, and not removable by averaging. It is the direct consequence of having stopped sampling k-space at a finite kmax.

Zero filling does not add resolution

Padding k-space with zeros before the FFT produces a smoothly interpolated image on a finer pixel grid. It genuinely helps — small structures are easier to see when they are not pixel-quantised, and partial-volume steps look less blocky — but it adds no information. The width of the point spread function is set by kmax, and zero filling does not change kmax. A 256² image zero-filled to 512² has 512² pixels and 256² worth of resolution. Manufacturers make heavy use of this; it is legitimate, and it is also why "matrix size" on a report is not the same as "resolution".

10.3  Magnitude, phase, and the noise you can see

Reconstruction yields a complex image. Clinical viewing almost always uses the magnitude, because phase is corrupted by B₀ inhomogeneity, coil geometry and eddy currents in ways that carry no anatomical meaning. But discarding phase has costs and consequences:

Magnitude√(I² + Q²)Everything routine. Noise becomes Rician, not Gaussian: in low-signal regions the mean is biased upward, so background is never truly zero and SNR estimates made naively are wrong.

10.4  When the samples are not on a grid

Radial, spiral and PROPELLER acquisitions sample k-space on trajectories that do not land on the Cartesian grid the FFT requires. The standard solution is gridding:

  1. Density compensation. Radial and spiral trajectories oversample the centre massively — every spoke passes through it. Each sample is weighted by the inverse of the local sampling density, otherwise the reconstruction is dominated by the centre and looks hopelessly blurred.
  2. Convolution onto the grid. Each sample is spread onto nearby Cartesian points with a compact kernel — a Kaiser–Bessel window is the standard choice because it concentrates energy well in both domains.
  3. Inverse FFT, usually onto an oversampled grid (typically 2×) to keep aliased side lobes of the kernel away from the field of view.
  4. Deapodisation. Divide the image by the transform of the gridding kernel to undo the shading it introduced.

Modern implementations wrap this into a non-uniform FFT (NUFFT) operator and use it inside an iterative reconstruction, which handles undersampling and coil sensitivities at the same time (Chapter 14).

The point where physics hands off to image processing

By the time an image reaches a PACS workstation it has been filtered, normalised, interpolated, warped and rescaled. For diagnosis that is all appropriate. For quantitative image processing it is a serious problem: intensities are not physical units, they are not comparable between scanners or even between sequences on the same scanner, and some of the operations are not documented. If you are building algorithms on MRI data, read Chapter 16 before trusting a single pixel value.

Key points

  • Reconstruction is an inverse FFT plus a long list of corrections for the ways reality violates its assumptions.
  • The PSF is the transform of the k-space window. Finite kmax ⇒ sinc PSF ⇒ Gibbs ringing.
  • Filtering trades ringing for blur; zero filling interpolates but adds no resolution.
  • Magnitude images have Rician noise with a positive bias that matters for quantitation.
  • Non-Cartesian data needs gridding: density compensation, kernel convolution, FFT, deapodisation.
  • Displayed intensities are not physical units — a fact that dominates Chapter 16.
