Part III · Making an Image

Spatial Encoding: Slice, Frequency and Phase

A receive coil produces one voltage. One number per instant, summed over every proton in range. Turning that into a picture requires making each location's contribution distinguishable — and the only handle we have is the Larmor equation. Everything in this chapter is an application of one idea: make the field depend on position, and frequency becomes a map.

7.1  The problem, stated precisely

Without gradients, every proton in the coil's sensitive volume precesses at the same frequency and contributes to the same signal, indistinguishably. The measured signal is

7.1 s(t) = ∫ ρ(r) e−iω(r)t dr If ω is the same everywhere, this integral collapses to a single number times a single oscillation — no spatial information whatsoever. Make ω depend on r and the integral becomes a transform we can invert.

With a gradient on, ω(r) = γ(B₀ + G·r). Dropping into the rotating frame at γB₀ to remove the carrier, the signal becomes:

7.2 s(t) = ∫ ρ(r) e−iγ(G·r)t dr   =   ∫ ρ(r) e−i2π k·r dr   with   k(t) = (γ/2π)∫0t G(t′) dt′ That is the Fourier transform of the spin density, evaluated at spatial frequency k. The scanner does not measure an image — it measures Fourier coefficients, one per sampling instant, and the gradient waveform determines which coefficient. Chapter 8 is entirely about this equation; here we build it up one axis at a time.

7.2  Slice selection: excite only one plane

Turn on a gradient along z during the RF pulse. Now every axial plane has its own Larmor frequency. A pulse containing only frequencies in a narrow band will tip only the plane whose frequencies fall in that band; everything else is off-resonance and, as Figure 3.1 showed, barely moves.

7.3 Δz = BWRF / (γ̄ · Gz)      zcentre = (fRF − f0) / (γ̄ · Gz) Two independent controls. Thickness comes from the ratio of pulse bandwidth to gradient strength — thinner slices need a stronger gradient or a longer (narrower-band) pulse. Position comes purely from the transmit centre frequency: to move the slice, you retune the radio, you do not move anything mechanical.
The rephasing lobe

While the slice gradient is on, spins across the thickness of the slice precess at different rates and accumulate a phase spread — by the end of the pulse the slice is dephased and would give almost no signal. Every slice-selective pulse is therefore followed by a negative gradient lobe of roughly half the area of the slice-select gradient, which unwinds that phase. If you look at any real sequence diagram and wonder why the slice gradient has a small negative kick after each RF pulse, that is what it is doing.

Slice thickness, gaps and cross-talk

  • Thin slices need strong gradients and give less signal (fewer spins per voxel), so there is an SNR floor: below about 2 mm on a 2D sequence you generally switch to a 3D acquisition instead.
  • Real slice profiles are not rectangles. The tails of one slice excite its neighbour, saturating it — cross-talk, which appears as alternating bright/dark slices. The fixes are a slice gap (typically 10–30% of thickness) or interleaved acquisition (excite all odd slices, then all even), which gives each neighbour time to recover.
  • 3D sequences excite a whole slab and then phase-encode along z as well, giving thin, contiguous, isotropic partitions with better SNR per unit time — at the cost of longer scans and wrap-around in the slab direction.

7.3  Frequency encoding: one axis for free

Once a slice is excited, turn on a gradient along x while sampling. Each column of the slice now precesses at its own frequency, and the coil sees the sum of all those tones. A Fourier transform of that composite signal separates them, and because frequency maps linearly to position, the spectrum is a projection of the object along x.

7.4  Phase encoding: the second axis costs you time

Why not simply apply a y gradient at the same time and read two frequencies at once? Because a single received tone cannot say whether it came from a high-x/low-y location or a low-x/high-y one — the mapping from 2D position to 1D frequency is many-to-one. We need an independent handle.

That handle is phase. Before the readout, turn a y gradient on for a fixed duration and then off. During that interval spins at different y precess at different rates, so they end up with a position-dependent phase which they retain afterwards. Repeat the whole experiment with a different gradient amplitude each time, and each repetition records the object under a different phase twist. Fourier-transforming across repetitions separates the y positions.

7.4 φ(y) = γ Gy τ y   ⟹   ky = (γ/2π) Gy τ Phase encoding and frequency encoding are the same operation; the only difference is whether you sample during the gradient (frequency) or after it (phase). Both simply move you to a position in k-space. Once you see this, Chapter 8 becomes obvious rather than mystical.

7.5  Putting it together: the spin-warp sequence

7.5 Tscan = TR × NPE × NEX   (2D, one echo per TR) 256 phase-encode steps at TR = 500 ms and one average is 128 seconds per slice group. This equation is the reason every acceleration technique in Chapters 11 and 14 exists — and note which term is missing: the number of frequency-encoded samples costs nothing, because they all come from one readout.

7.6  Three dimensions, and oblique slices

3D acquisition replaces the thin slice with a thick slab and adds a second phase-encoding axis along z. Scan time becomes TR × NPE,y × NPE,z × NEX, which is why 3D sequences are almost always gradient-echo with very short TR. In exchange you get isotropic sub-millimetre voxels that can be reformatted in any plane, and an SNR advantage of √Nz because every excitation collects signal from the whole slab.

Oblique slices require no new hardware. The three gradient coils are combined: a slice normal n̂ is produced by driving Gx, Gy and Gz in proportion to the components of n̂. Any plane in space is reachable by a 3×3 rotation matrix applied to the logical gradient waveforms — one of the quiet advantages MRI holds over CT, where the plane is fixed by the geometry of the gantry.

Logical versus physical axes

Sequence programmers work in logical axes — slice, phase, read. The scanner maps these onto physical x, y, z through the rotation matrix for the chosen orientation. Artifacts, however, follow the logical axes: motion ghosts always propagate along the phase-encode direction, chemical shift displacement always along the readout direction, no matter how the slice is angled. When you are diagnosing an artifact, the first question is always “which way was phase encode?”

Key points

  • Gradients turn position into frequency: ω(r) = γ(B₀ + G·r). That is the entire encoding principle.
  • Slice thickness = RF bandwidth ÷ (γ̄ × Gz); slice position = transmit frequency offset.
  • Frequency encoding is free — one readout gives a whole axis. Phase encoding costs a TR per line.
  • Phase and frequency encoding are the same physics, differing only in whether you sample during the gradient.
  • Scan time = TR × NPE × NEX, independent of readout matrix size.
  • The centre of k-space carries contrast and brightness; the periphery carries edges.
  • Artifacts follow logical axes, not anatomical ones.
